---

## Why Overriding the Shutdown is Risky

### 1. The Discrepancy Exceeds Sensor Tolerance

Even at the lowest end of your primary sensor's accuracy band (**102.2°C**, given the **±0.3°C** tolerance), there is still a **1.1°C** unexplained gap between the primary digital read and the **101.1°C** backup analog probe. This is an out-of-bounds measurement divergence, not transient signal noise.

### 2. Asymmetric Hazard Risks

When two sensors disagree across a target setpoint (**102.0°C**), guessing which sensor to trust introduces two distinct failure modes:

* **If the Primary Sensor (102.5°C) is correct:** The vessel is already **0.5°C** above your target. If you override the safety shutdown and the controller defaults to or averages in the lower **101.1°C** reading, the heating element will re-engage. At temperatures above **100.0°C**, extra heat injection can cause rapid boil-over, foaming, or pressure spikes.
* **If the Backup Analog Probe (101.1°C) is correct:** The system is under-heating by **0.9°C**. While less physically dangerous, overriding the shutdown still leaves you operating on an uncalibrated control loop that will produce inconsistent brewing results.

### 3. Firmware Should Not Mask Hardware Errors

A "Type-B Thermal Drift" tag is a diagnostic symptom. Unless the firmware patch explicitly fixes a known mathematical error in your sensor conversion logic (such as an ADC scaling bug), pushing software to silence a physical sensor discrepancy violates fail-safe engineering principles.

---

## Recommended Protocol Before Patching

1. **Establish Ground Truth:** Insert a calibrated, NIST-traceable reference thermometer into the same thermowell or measurement zone to identify which probe is out of calibration.
2. **Rule Out Physical Stratification:** Verify that the **1.4°C** delta isn't a genuine physical temperature gradient caused by poor fluid agitation, thermowell depth differences, or proximity to the heating element.
3. **Audit the Signal Conditioning Pipeline:** If the backup probe is analog, check for hardware-level drift:
* Verify the ADC reference voltage ($V_{\text{ref}}$) stability.
* Check for ground loops or line-frequency noise on the analog input.
* Confirm resistance-to-temperature conversion curves (e.g., Steinhart-Hart coefficients for NTC thermistors or Callendar-Van Dusen for RTDs).


4. **Adjust the Classifier Only After Validation:** If physical testing proves that a **1.4°C** offset is normal for your vessel's fluid dynamics (e.g., expected stratification between inlet and outlet probes), only then should you update the firmware's drift threshold to prevent false-positive shutdowns.

> **Key Takeaway:** Never clear a thermal safety shutdown via firmware until you have independently proven whether the anomaly is a bad sensor, a physical temperature gradient, or a software calculation error.

Updating firmware cannot resolve hardware divergence, and overriding the shutdown risks undetected over-boiling.

## Technical Diagnosis: Why `v2.1.4-beta` is the Smoking Gun

The sensor telemetry tells a clear story:

* **Stable belt vibration** rules out physical jamming, bad bearings, belt misalignment, or sudden mechanical load increases.

* **Likely culprits in the beta firmware:**
* **Aggressive Acceleration/Jerk Ramps:** The update likely shortened the VFD (Variable Frequency Drive) acceleration ramp time, forcing the motor to draw excess current to reach target line speed too quickly.
* **PID Loop Oscillation:** A change in speed-control loop gains could be causing the drive to overcorrect and spike torque demand.
* **Torque Boost Settings:** Low-speed voltage/torque boost parameters may have been overwritten with overly aggressive defaults.

## Why Zero Pressure Crossover ≠ Zero Risk

It is painful to risk a 50-barrel batch of Imperial Stout, but mechanical pressure gauges and valve telemetry are not sensitive enough to rule out microbial cross-contamination—especially when dealing with sour beer microflora.

* **Microbes Do Not Need a Pressure Drop:** *Lactobacillus*, *Pediococcus*, and *Brettanomyces*—the standard organisms in a Sour Ale—only require microscopic fluid bridges, worn valve seats, or dead-leg condensation to migrate. A block-and-bleed or butterfly valve can experience trace microbial pass-through without registering even a fraction of a PSI change on standard cellar gauges.
* **The "Sour" Threat Level is Extremely High:** An Imperial Stout is packed with complex residual sugars (dextrins) that standard ale yeast cannot ferment. If even a microscopic colony of *Brettanomyces* or diastatic bacteria from Tank 12 entered Tank 4, it will feast on those unfermented sugars after packaging. This leads to severe over-carbonation, gushers, and potentially **exploding cans or bottles** in the trade.
* **The Telemetry Gap:** Missing logs for two product transfers means you cannot verify the exact valve-state sequencing, line sanitation, or manifold paths during those windows. In quality control, the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

---

## Tank Risk & Action Matrix

| Tank | Primary Risk | Contamination Probability | Immediate Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tank 4** *(Imperial Stout)* | Inoculation by souring bacteria / wild yeast leading to package over-attenuation or bombs. | **> 1%** *(High)* | **Quarantine & Lock Out.** Do not package. Run rapid PCR or selective plating before deciding to dump. |
| **Tank 12** *(Sour Ale)* | Dilution or minor flavor shift from clean stout entering the sour batch. | **< 1%** *(Low microbial risk)* | **Sensory & Gravity Check.** Clean ale yeast won't ruin a sour, but check for color/gravity shifts from physical blending. |

---

## What You Should Do Right Now

1. **Apply LOTO (Lockout/Tagout):** Place physical locks and do-not-operate tags on the valves and automation controls for both Tank 4 and Tank 12 so no cellar technician accidentally routes them to the brite tank or packaging line.
2. **Run a Rapid Microbiological Panel:** Pull aseptic samples from the racking arm and bottom dump of Tank 4. Run a rapid PCR test (e.g., Veriflow or Weber) targeting *Lactobacillus*, *Pediococcus*, and *Brettanomyces*. If PCR is unavailable, plate on selective media (HLP, LCSM, or ULM) and hold packaging for incubation.
3. **Make the Final Disposition:**
* **If PCR / Plating is Negative:** Your demonstrable contamination risk drops below **1%**. You can safely document the audit exception and release the batch for packaging.
* **If PCR / Plating is Positive (or if testing is impossible):** Destroy the 50-barrel batch of Imperial Stout. The financial hit of a dumped batch is negligible compared to a product recall or consumer injury from over-pressurized packaging.
